SELF-ATTENTION
================================================================================

1. The Problem: One Word, Many Meanings
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The embedding layer is a lookup table: ONE row per word in the vocabulary
(see 01_word_token_vector.txt). Every time a word appears we fetch the same row,
fixed during training, before this sentence existed.

Now read these two sentences:

    Sentence 1:  money bank grows
    Sentence 2:  river bank flows

"bank" is a financial institution in the first and the edge of a river in the
second. The lookup table hands us the same vector both times.

The model receives identical input for two different meanings. No later layer
can recover the difference, because it was never in the input.

This is not an edge case. "apple" is a fruit or a company. "it" points to a
different noun in every sentence. "not" flips a word that may sit far away.

Vectors that never change are called STATIC embeddings (Word2Vec, GloVe,
FastText): one word, one fixed vector, chosen without seeing the sentence.
That is a dictionary entry. But we do not read dictionary entries, we read
sentences.


1.1 What We Actually Need
--------------------------

The vector for "bank" must be DIFFERENT in the two sentences. A vector that
depends on the sentence around it is called a CONTEXTUAL embedding.

Notice how you disambiguated "bank" yourself: you used the other words.
"money" told you finance, "river" told you water. So the recipe is:

    the new vector for a word
        = a blend of the sentence's word vectors,
          each contributing in proportion to how relevant it is

For word i:

    new_i = sum over j of ( w_ij * V_j )

    w_ij = how much word i pays attention to word j
    V_j  = the vector word j contributes when attended to (derived from j's
           static embedding, not the embedding itself; see section 3)

The weights are positive and sum to 1 across j, which makes each new vector a
proper weighted average.


1.2 Watching It Work
---------------------

Every word gets its own row of weights. Here is the row for "bank" in each
sentence:

    Sentence 1:  bank -> 0.25 money   0.70 bank   0.05 grows
                 new_bank = 0.25*V_money + 0.70*V_bank + 0.05*V_grows

    Sentence 2:  bank -> 0.20 river   0.78 bank   0.02 flows
                 new_bank = 0.20*V_river + 0.78*V_bank + 0.02*V_flows

The row pulled from the lookup table was identical. The two results are not,
because a quarter of the first blend came from "money" and a fifth of the
second came from "river".

    Static embedding     = meaning in the dictionary
    Contextual embedding = meaning in this sentence

This is SELF-ATTENTION. "Attention" because each word decides how much to look
at every other word; "self" because those words are in the same sentence.

All of it assumed the weights w_ij were handed to us. They are not. Producing
them is the whole problem:

    When understanding this word, which other words should I look at,
    and by how much?

Sections 3 to 5 answer that. But first: we brought in context by looking at
every word at once. There is an older and more obvious way to do it, and it is
worth seeing why it was abandoned.

================================================================================

2. Why Not Just Read the Sentence in Order?
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Section 1 asked for context. There is an obvious way to get it: read the sentence
one word at a time, carrying along everything seen so far. That is a Recurrent
Neural Network (RNN), and it was the standard answer for years.


2.1 What Recurrence Does
------------------------

An RNN keeps one vector, the hidden state h, and updates it at every word:

    h_t = f( h_(t-1) , x_t )

    x_t = embedding of the word at position t
    h_t = everything understood up to position t

Reading "money bank grows":

    h_1 = f(h_0, money)     has seen: money
    h_2 = f(h_1, bank)      has seen: money bank
    h_3 = f(h_2, grows)     has seen: money bank grows

This genuinely solves section 1. h_2 represents "bank" after "money" was read, so
it is no longer the fixed dictionary vector. Word order comes for free too, since
a word's position is just its step number.

Recurrence works. The problem is what it costs.


2.2 Failure 1 - A Word Only Sees Its Left Side
----------------------------------------------

h_t is built from words 1..t, so word t cannot use anything after it.

    "money bank grows"        "money" is first and gets no context at all
    "the bank of the river"   when "bank" is processed, "river" is unread

Meaning does not respect reading order. The clue that disambiguates a word sits
to its right just as often as to its left.

The usual patch is a bidirectional RNN: run a second RNN right-to-left and
concatenate the two hidden states. That gives each word a left summary and a
right summary glued together - two one-sided views, never a single view in which
"bank" weighs "money" and "river" against each other at once.


2.3 Failure 2 - Distance Weakens the Link
-----------------------------------------

For word 1 to influence word 50, its signal passes through 49 updates of h, each
one multiplying and squashing it. The path length between two words is their
distance in the sentence.

Training sends the gradient back along that same path, shrinking it at every
step. This is the vanishing gradient problem, and it means the pairs that train
worst are the ones furthest apart - exactly where context is hardest to guess.

LSTMs and GRUs add gates that let the state pass through more cleanly, which
stretches the usable range. It does not remove the limit: the path is still 49
steps long, so influence still fades as sentences grow.


2.4 Failure 3 - No Parallelism
------------------------------

h_t needs h_(t-1). Word 10 cannot be computed until word 9 is finished, so a
sentence of length n takes n sequential steps regardless of available hardware.

GPUs are built for large parallel matrix multiplications and sit mostly idle
waiting. Training time is bounded by sentence length rather than by compute.


2.5 The Escape
--------------

All three failures share one cause: information travels between words only by
passing step by step through h.

Attention was first bolted onto RNNs to relieve this. In translation, the encoder
had to squeeze an entire sentence into one final hidden state before the decoder
saw it; attention let the decoder look back at every encoder state instead of
that single summary. The Transformer took the stronger position - remove
recurrence entirely and let attention carry all movement of information. Hence
the paper's title, "Attention Is All You Need".

    RNN         word1 -> word2 -> word3 -> word4     n steps, one direction
    Attention   [word1, word2, word3, word4]         1 step, every pair

Every pair of words is now one operation apart in both directions, and all words
are computed at the same time.


2.6 The Price
-------------

Two things are lost, and much of the architecture exists to pay for them.

Order. Recurrence encoded position for free through the step number. Processing
all words at once treats the sentence as a bag of words, so "Moaz killed Lion"
and "Lion killed Moaz" look identical. Position has to be added back explicitly
(see 03_positional_encoding.txt).

Cost. Connecting every word to every other word means n x n comparisons instead
of n steps. Section 8 works out what that costs.

================================================================================

3. Introducing Q, K, V (Query, Key, Value)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Section 1 left one thing undefined: the weight w_ij, how much word i should pay
attention to word j. Everything else followed from it.

To produce that weight we need a score for "how relevant is word j to word i".
We already have a vector per word, so the obvious move is to compare the
embeddings directly:

    score(i, j) = E_i . E_j

That fails for three reasons.

Relevance is directional, a dot product is not.
    E_i . E_j is always equal to E_j . E_i. So "bank" attending to "river" would
    be forced to equal "river" attending to "bank". Those are not the same need:
    "bank" is ambiguous and depends heavily on "river", while "river" is already
    clear and gains little from "bank".

One vector cannot hold two jobs.
    The vector used to decide relevance would also be the vector copied into the
    blend. But the features that make a word a good MATCH are not the features
    worth CONTRIBUTING. "river" is relevant to "bank" because it is a body of
    water; what it contributes is the water sense of the sentence. Tying the two
    together means improving one damages the other.

Nothing is learnable.
    Fixed embeddings give a fixed similarity. The model has no way to learn what
    "relevant" should mean for the task it is being trained on.


3.1 Three Roles, Three Vectors
------------------------------

The fix is to derive three different vectors from each word's embedding, one per
role:

    Query (Q)   what this word is looking for
                "bank" asks: am I financial or geographic? what would settle it?

    Key (K)     what this word advertises to others
                "river" offers: I am about water and nature.
                "money" offers: I am about finance.

    Value (V)   what this word contributes if it is chosen
                the actual meaning that gets mixed into the blend

A word plays all three roles at once: it asks with its Query, is matched against
with its Key, and gives with its Value. The names come from database lookup -
you present a query, it is matched against keys, and you get back the value.

Matching Q against K produces w_ij; V is what those weights are then applied to.


3.2 How They Are Produced
-------------------------

Each role is a learned linear projection of the embedding:

    Q = X W_Q
    K = X W_K
    V = X W_V

With sequence length n and embedding size d_model:

    X               (n x d_model)     the input embeddings, one row per word
    W_Q, W_K        (d_model x d_k)
    W_V             (d_model x d_v)
    Q, K            (n x d_k)
    V               (n x d_v)

Two points about these matrices:

- They are learned along with the rest of the model. What counts as "relevant"
  is not hand-designed; it is trained.
- There is one W_Q, one W_K, one W_V for the whole layer, shared by every
  position. Parameter count does not grow with sentence length, and the same
  notion of relevance applies everywhere in the sentence.

d_q must equal d_k, since Q and K are compared with a dot product and the two
vectors must have matching length. d_v is free - V is never compared to anything,
only summed - though in practice d_v = d_k. Section 5 returns to this.

This also settles the loose end from section 1: V_j is the row of V for word j,
that is, word j's static embedding passed through W_V. Not the embedding itself.

The weights w_ij still have to be computed from Q and K. That is section 4.

================================================================================

4. How Attention Scores Are Calculated (Q, K -> Weights -> V)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Section 3 produced Q, K and V. What remains is the weight w_ij from section 1,
in four steps:

    score  ->  scale  ->  normalize  ->  blend

Worked through on "river bank flows", focusing on the word "bank".


4.1 Step 1 - Score Every Pair
-----------------------------

The comparison between a Query and a Key is a dot product:

    s_ij = q_i . k_j

A dot product is large and positive when two vectors point the same way, near
zero when they are unrelated, and negative when they point in opposite
directions. That is exactly the ordering we want from a relevance score, and it
costs one multiply-add per dimension.

Doing this for every pair at once is a single matrix multiplication:

    S = Q K^T          (n x d_k) times (d_k x n)  ->  (n x n)

Row i of S holds word i's scores against every word in the sentence, including
itself - a word is usually its own strongest match, and should be.

Note: 
-------------------
S is not symmetric. Entry (i,j) uses q_i and k_j, entry (j,i) uses q_j and k_i,
and Q and K come from different projections. This is the asymmetry that section 3
required: "bank" can depend heavily on "river" while "river" barely depends on
"bank".

For "bank" in "river bank flows", suppose the row comes out as:

    s(bank, river) = 24.0
    s(bank, bank)  = 34.9
    s(bank, flows) =  5.5


4.2 Step 2 - Scale by sqrt(d_k)
-------------------------------

Before anything else is done with S, it is divided by sqrt(d_k):

    S_scaled = S / sqrt(d_k)

With d_k = 64 the divisor is 8, so the row above becomes:

    3.00,  4.36,  0.69

Section 5 explains why this division is necessary. For now, take it as part of
the pipeline.


4.3 Step 3 - Normalize With Softmax
-----------------------------------

Scores are unbounded and can be negative, so they cannot be used as weights
directly. Section 1 required weights that are positive and sum to 1.

The obvious fix, dividing each score by the row's sum, does not work: with mixed
signs the sum can be near zero or negative, which makes the weights explode or
come out negative. Negative attention has no meaning here - a word cannot
contribute a negative amount of itself.

Softmax exponentiates first, which forces every value positive while preserving
their order, then normalizes:

    softmax(s)_j = exp(s_j) / sum over k of exp(s_k)

It is applied ROW-WISE. Each row of S becomes a set of weights summing to 1;
the matrix as a whole does not sum to 1.

    A = softmax_rows( S_scaled )      (n x n)

For the "bank" row:

    exp(3.00) = 20.1     ->  w(bank, river) = 0.20
    exp(4.36) = 78.3     ->  w(bank, bank)  = 0.78
    exp(0.69) =  2.0     ->  w(bank, flows) = 0.02
    sum       = 100.4

These are the weights section 1 assumed. They are now derived rather than given.

Note that exponentiating widens gaps: a score 1.36 higher than another becomes a
weight about 4 times larger. Softmax does not just normalize, it sharpens. How
sharp depends on the size of the scores, which is what section 5 is about.


4.4 Step 4 - Blend the Values
-----------------------------

The weights now multiply the Value vectors, which is the update rule from
section 1:

    y_i = sum over j of ( w_ij * v_j )

    y_bank = 0.20 * V_river + 0.78 * V_bank + 0.02 * V_flows

In matrix form the whole sentence is done at once:

    Y = A V            (n x n) times (n x d_v)  ->  (n x d_v)

Row i of Y is word i's contextual embedding, and it replaces the static
embedding that entered the layer.

Putting the four steps together:

    Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q K^T / sqrt(d_k) ) V

Shapes end to end:

    X                (n x d_model)
    Q, K             (n x d_k)
    V                (n x d_v)
    S = Q K^T        (n x n)
    A = softmax(.)   (n x n)
    Y = A V          (n x d_v)

The weighted sum is what makes this trainable: it is smooth and differentiable,
so gradients flow back through the weights into W_Q, W_K and W_V, and the model
learns what "relevant" should mean.

================================================================================

5. Why We Divide by sqrt(d_k) (Scaled Dot-Product Attention)
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Step 2 of section 4 divided every score by sqrt(d_k) without saying why. The
reason is that the size of a dot product depends on how many dimensions it sums
over, and softmax reacts badly to large numbers.


5.1 Where Large Scores Come From
--------------------------------

A score is a dot product over d_k dimensions:

    s_ij = q_i . k_j = q_1*k_1 + q_2*k_2 + ... + q_dk*k_dk

That is a sum of d_k terms. If the components are roughly independent with mean 0
and variance 1, the variance of the sum is d_k, so its typical size is:

    standard deviation = sqrt(d_k)

    d_k = 64   ->  scores spread by about 8
    d_k = 512  ->  scores spread by about 23

Nothing about meaning changed here. Widening the head from 64 to 512 dimensions
multiplies every score by roughly 3 purely because more terms are being added.
The scale of the scores is an accident of dimension.


5.2 Why Large Scores Break Softmax
----------------------------------

Section 4.3 noted that softmax sharpens: it exponentiates, so gaps between
scores turn into ratios between weights. When scores spread by sqrt(d_k), the
gaps do too, and exponentiating a large gap destroys the mixture.

Take the raw scores from section 4 and skip the scaling:

    unscaled   24.0,  34.9,  5.5   ->  w = 0.00002,  0.99998,  0.00000
    scaled      3.00,  4.36, 0.69  ->  w = 0.20,     0.78,     0.02

Unscaled, "bank" attends only to itself. Its output becomes a copy of its own
Value vector, which is just its static embedding passed through W_V. Self-
attention collapses into the fixed dictionary vector of section 1 - the exact
problem it was built to solve.

Training is hurt worse than inference. The gradient of softmax approaches zero
when its outputs approach 0 or 1, so a saturated row passes almost no signal
back to W_Q and W_K. The model cannot learn what "relevant" means, because the
weights that decide relevance stop receiving gradient.

Note how this failure scales: the bigger the model, the larger d_k, the sharper
the saturation. Without a fix, the architecture would get worse as it grew.


5.3 The Fix, and Why It Is sqrt(d_k)
------------------------------------

Divide the scores by sqrt(d_k) before softmax:

    variance(s / sqrt(d_k)) = variance(s) / d_k = d_k / d_k = 1

    d_k = 64   ->  divide by 8     ->  spread back to about 1
    d_k = 512  ->  divide by 23    ->  spread back to about 1

Softmax now sees inputs on the same scale no matter how wide the head is. How
sharp the attention becomes is decided by real differences in relevance, not by
the dimension count.

Why not divide by d_k instead? That over-corrects. The spread would become
1/sqrt(d_k), shrinking toward zero as models grow, and softmax fed near-identical
inputs returns near-uniform weights - every word attended to equally, which is no
attention at all. Too large a divisor is as damaging as none. Only sqrt(d_k)
leaves the variance at 1.

Why d_k and not d_v? Only Q and K enter the dot product, and d_k is the number of
terms in that sum. V is never compared to anything, so d_v is irrelevant here.
And d_q needs no separate mention: section 3.2 already established that d_q = d_k
is forced, since the dot product requires both vectors to have the same length.

This single division is why the mechanism is called Scaled Dot-Product Attention:

    Attention(Q, K, V) = softmax( Q K^T / sqrt(d_k) ) V

6. Worked Example, End to End
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Sections 3 to 5 built the mechanism in symbols. Here it runs on real numbers,
small enough to check by hand.

The word under test is "apple", which is either a fruit or a company. We will
run it through two sentences and watch the same input row come out as two
different vectors.


6.1 Setup
---------

Sentence:   apple launched phones      n = 3, d_model = 2, d_k = d_v = 2

To make the result readable, give the two dimensions a meaning:

    dimension 1 = how food-like the word is
    dimension 2 = how tech-like the word is

Real models do not have interpretable dimensions like this - meaning is spread
across all d_model of them. This is a reading aid, not a claim about how
embeddings work.

The static embeddings, straight from the lookup table:

              food  tech
    apple    [  1     1  ]     ambiguous: equally food and tech
    launched [  0     1  ]
    phones   [  0     2  ]

    X = [ 1  1 ]
        [ 0  1 ]
        [ 0  2 ]

The three projection matrices. In a trained model these are learned; here they
are picked to keep the arithmetic small.

    W_Q = [ 1  0 ]      W_K = [ 1  0 ]      W_V = [ 1  0 ]
          [ 0  1 ]            [ 2  1 ]            [ 0  2 ]


6.2 Step 1 - Project into Q, K, V
---------------------------------

    Q = X W_Q = [ 1  1 ]        K = X W_K = [ 3  1 ]        V = X W_V = [ 1  2 ]
                [ 0  1 ]                    [ 2  1 ]                    [ 0  2 ]
                [ 0  2 ]                    [ 4  2 ]                    [ 0  4 ]

Same three words, three different views. W_Q happens to be the identity here, so
Q equals X; K and V are genuinely different from the embeddings and from each
other, which is the point of section 3.


6.3 Step 2 - Score Every Pair
-----------------------------

    S = Q K^T

              apple  launched  phones
    apple   [   4        3        6   ]
    launched[   1        1        2   ]
    phones  [   2        2        4   ]

Read the top row: "apple" scores 4 against itself, 3 against "launched", 6
against "phones".

Note S is not symmetric. S[apple][launched] = 3 but S[launched][apple] = 1.
"apple" finds "launched" three times more worth attending to than "launched"
finds "apple". A plain dot product of embeddings could never produce that, which
is exactly the argument section 3 made for splitting Q from K.


6.4 Step 3 - Scale
------------------

Divide by sqrt(d_k) = sqrt(2) = 1.414:

    apple row:  4 / 1.414 = 2.83     3 / 1.414 = 2.12     6 / 1.414 = 4.24


6.5 Step 4 - Softmax the Row
----------------------------

    exp(2.83) = 16.91      ->  16.91 / 94.92 = 0.18   apple
    exp(2.12) =  8.34      ->   8.34 / 94.92 = 0.09   launched
    exp(4.24) = 69.66      ->  69.66 / 94.92 = 0.73   phones
    sum       = 94.92                          ----
                                               1.00

Doing the same for the other two rows gives the full attention matrix:

              apple  launched  phones
    apple   [  0.18    0.09     0.73  ]
    launched[  0.25    0.25     0.50  ]
    phones  [  0.16    0.16     0.67  ]

Every row sums to 1. The matrix as a whole does not.


6.6 Step 5 - Blend the Values
-----------------------------

    y_apple = 0.18 * V_apple + 0.09 * V_launched + 0.73 * V_phones
            = 0.18 * [1, 2]  + 0.09 * [0, 2]     + 0.73 * [0, 4]
            = [0.18, 0.36]   + [0, 0.18]         + [0, 2.92]
            = [0.18, 3.47]

All three rows at once, Y = A V:

              food   tech
    apple   [ 0.18   3.47 ]
    launched[ 0.25   3.01 ]
    phones  [ 0.16   3.35 ]


6.7 Reading the Result
----------------------

Follow "apple" through the layer:

    went in as   [ 1.00, 1.00 ]     food = tech, completely ambiguous
    came out as  [ 0.18, 3.47 ]     tech is 19x food

Nothing told the model that this "apple" is a company. It gave 0.73 of its
attention to "phones", and "phones" contributes a strongly tech Value, so the
tech dimension dominates the blend while the food dimension shrinks to almost
nothing.


6.8 The Same Word in a Different Sentence
-----------------------------------------

Now run "apple tastes sweet" through the identical layer - same W_Q, W_K, W_V,
same lookup table. Only the neighbours change:

    apple  [ 1  1 ]     the identical row as before
    tastes [ 2  0 ]
    sweet  [ 3  0 ]

The apple row of the score matrix comes out as 4, 2, 3, which scales to
2.83, 1.41, 2.12 and softmaxes to:

    0.58 apple     0.14 tastes     0.28 sweet

    y_apple = 0.58 * [1, 2] + 0.14 * [2, 0] + 0.28 * [3, 0]
            = [1.71, 1.15]

Side by side:

    static "apple"              [ 1.00, 1.00 ]    one row in the table

    in "apple launched phones"  [ 0.18, 3.47 ]    tech
    in "apple tastes sweet"     [ 1.71, 1.15 ]    food

One embedding row, one set of weights, two sentences, two different meanings.
This is the claim section 1 made with invented numbers, now produced by the
mechanism itself.

7. Masking: Blocking Pairs
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

So far every word attends to every word. Two situations require some pairs to be
switched off.


7.1 Why Pairs Must Be Blocked
-----------------------------

Padding. GPUs process sentences in batches, and a batch has to be one rectangular
tensor, so every sentence in it must have the same length. Short sentences are
filled out with a placeholder token, <PAD>:

    "apple launched phones"   ->  apple  launched  phones
    "apple fell"              ->  apple  fell      <PAD>

<PAD> carries no meaning. Left unblocked, it still gets a Key, still receives a
score, and still contributes its Value to the blend. Real words would be mixed
with a token that exists purely to square off a tensor.

Future words. When the model generates text one token at a time, position t must
not see positions after t, or it would be predicting a word it has already been
shown. That mask is the subject of 08_masked_multihead_attention.txt; the mechanism
below is the same one it uses.


7.2 How Blocking Works
----------------------

Add a mask matrix M to the scaled scores, before softmax:

    M[i][j] =    0     word i may attend to word j
    M[i][j] = -inf     blocked

    A = softmax( Q K^T / sqrt(d_k)  +  M )

Since exp(-inf) = 0, a blocked pair gets a weight of exactly zero, and it drops
out of the sum in the denominator as well - so the surviving weights still add up
to 1.

Why not simply zero the weights after softmax? Because then the row no longer
sums to 1. Zeroing 0.30 of a row leaves the remaining weights summing to 0.70,
and the output is no longer a weighted average - it is shrunk toward zero by
however much was masked, and by a different amount in every row. Section 1
required the weights to be a proper average. Adding -inf beforehand gets the
renormalization for free.

In code the value used is a large negative number such as -1e9 rather than true
negative infinity, because 0 * inf produces NaN in some operations.


7.3 The Padding Mask
--------------------

For "apple fell <PAD>", position 3 is padding, so column 3 is blocked for every
row:

              apple  fell  <PAD>
    apple   [   0     0    -inf ]
    fell    [   0     0    -inf ]
    <PAD>   [   0     0    -inf ]

Columns, not rows: the rule is that nothing may attend TO padding. The <PAD> row
still computes an output, which is harmless because it is discarded before the
loss is calculated.

The padding mask depends only on which positions are padding, so it is one
pattern per sentence, broadcast across all rows. It applies wherever real
sentences are batched, encoder self-attention included - it is not a
decoder-only concern.

8. What It Costs: Time and Space
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Section 2.6 promised an accounting of what "connect every word to every word"
costs. Rule of thumb: multiplying (A x B) by (B x C) takes about A * B * C
operations.


8.1 Time
--------

Three multiplications dominate, using d for d_model and taking d_k = d_v = d:

    Projections     X (n x d) times W (d x d), three times      O(n d^2)
    Scores          Q (n x d) times K^T (d x n)                 O(n^2 d)
    Blend           A (n x n) times V (n x d)                   O(n^2 d)

    total: O(n d^2 + n^2 d)

Which term dominates depends on whether the sentence is longer than the model is
wide. Once n > d, the n^2 terms win, and the layer is O(n^2 d).

The n^2 is quadratic, so doubling the sequence length quadruples the work. Ten
times the text is a hundred times the cost.


8.2 Space, Which Is the Harder Limit
------------------------------------

Softmax needs a whole row of scores at once to compute its denominator, so the
n x n matrix has to actually exist in memory - one per head, per layer. Training
must keep it for the backward pass as well.

At n = 100,000 tokens, in 32-bit floats:

    100,000 x 100,000 x 4 bytes = 40 GB

for a single attention matrix in a single layer. Time complexity makes long
sequences slow; space complexity makes them impossible. The failure mode is not
a long wait, it is an out-of-memory crash before the first step finishes.

This is why the practical fixes target memory first. FlashAttention computes the
same result without ever materializing the full matrix, by processing it in tiles
and recomputing what it needs during the backward pass. Sparse and linear
attention change the math so that fewer than n^2 pairs are scored. Architectures
like Mamba drop attention altogether.


8.3 Compared With Recurrence
----------------------------

Section 2 argued the trade-off; these are the numbers behind it.

                          RNN            Self-attention
    total operations      O(n d^2)       O(n^2 d)
    sequential steps      O(n)           O(1)
    path between words    O(n)           O(1)
    memory                O(n d)         O(n^2)

Self-attention does more arithmetic and uses far more memory, and buys two
things with it: every word computed at once, and every pair one step apart.

The balance has shifted since 2017. Then, n was around 512 and d around 512 to
1024, so O(n d^2) was the larger term and the quadratic cost was not the
constraint. Now sequences run to hundreds of thousands of tokens while d has
grown slowly, so n^2 d dominates everything.

9. Where This Goes Next
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

What has been built is one attention pattern over one sequence. Three limits
remain, each handled by another note.

It has no idea of order. Permute the rows of X and the rows of the output are
permuted the same way, with identical values - the mechanism is permutation
equivariant. "Moaz killed Lion" and "Lion killed Moaz" produce the same set of
vectors, just reordered, because nothing in Q K^T or the blend refers to
position. Section 2.6 flagged this as the price of dropping recurrence.
    -> 03_positional_encoding.txt

One set of W_Q, W_K, W_V produces one notion of relevance per layer, so a single
layer cannot track grammatical agreement and topical similarity at the same time.
Running several attentions in parallel over slices of d_model fixes it.
    -> 04_multihead_attention.txt

Q, K and V all come from the same sequence, which is what "self" means. Taking Q
from one sequence and K, V from another is what connects an encoder to a decoder.
    -> 09_cross_attention.txt

The causal mask that section 7 deferred.
    -> 08_masked_multihead_attention.txt

10. Practice Questions
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1: Given sequence length n, model width d_model, and per-head sizes d_k and
    d_v, what are the shapes of X, Q, K, V, S, A and Y?
A:  X (n x d_model), Q and K (n x d_k), V (n x d_v), S and A (n x n),
    Y (n x d_v). W_Q and W_K are (d_model x d_k), W_V is (d_model x d_v).

Q2: Why must d_q equal d_k, while d_v is free to differ?
A:  Q and K meet in a dot product, which requires both vectors to have the same
    length. V never enters that product - it is only summed with weights that
    have already been computed - so its width only has to match whatever comes
    after the layer.

Q3: Why not skip W_Q and W_K and score words by the dot product of their
    embeddings directly?
A:  E_i . E_j is symmetric, so word i's attention to word j would be forced to
    equal j's attention to i. Relevance is not mutual: an ambiguous word depends
    heavily on the word that disambiguates it, not the reverse. Separate
    projections make S asymmetric. A single vector would also have to serve as
    both the thing matched and the thing contributed, and nothing would be
    learnable.

Q4: What happens to training if the division by sqrt(d_k) is removed?
A:  Scores are a sum of d_k terms, so their spread grows like sqrt(d_k).
    Softmax turns that spread into near one-hot weights, one word takes almost
    all the attention, and the output collapses to a copy of a single Value
    vector. Softmax's gradient is near zero when its outputs are near 0 or 1, so
    W_Q and W_K stop receiving useful gradient and the model cannot learn what
    relevance means.

Q5: Why sqrt(d_k) and not d_k?
A:  Dividing by d_k over-corrects: the spread becomes 1/sqrt(d_k), which shrinks
    as models grow, and softmax over near-identical inputs returns near-uniform
    weights - every word attended to equally, which is no attention at all. Only
    sqrt(d_k) leaves the variance at 1.

Q6: Swap words 2 and 5 in the input. What happens to the output?
A:  Rows 2 and 5 of the output swap, and every value is unchanged. Self-attention
    is permutation equivariant, not invariant: the outputs move with the inputs
    rather than staying fixed. Nothing in the mechanism refers to position, which
    is why positional information has to be added to the embeddings beforehand.

Q7: Why is a mask added as -inf before softmax rather than by zeroing the
    weights afterwards?
A:  Adding -inf removes the blocked term from the softmax denominator too, so the
    surviving weights still sum to 1. Zeroing afterwards leaves the row summing
    to less than 1, which shrinks the output toward zero by an amount that
    differs from row to row, and it is no longer a weighted average.

Q8: Self-attention is O(n^2 d) in time and O(n^2) in space. Which limit is hit
    first in practice, and why?
A:  Space. The n x n matrix must be materialized for softmax and retained for the
    backward pass - at n = 100,000 in fp32 that is 40 GB for one matrix in one
    layer. Excess time cost produces a slow run; excess memory produces an
    out-of-memory crash. This is why FlashAttention targets materialization
    rather than arithmetic.
