ADD & NORM
================================================================================

Notes 02 and 04 built one sublayer. This note is about the wrapper placed around
every sublayer in the model, and why a stack of them cannot be trained without
it.

    Add & Norm  =  a residual connection, then layer normalization

Two mechanisms fixing two different problems. Taken one at a time.


1. The Problem: Deep Stacks Stop Training
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

The encoder is 6 identical blocks stacked; modern models use 12, 48 or more. The
reasonable expectation is that more layers can only help. If the extra layers
turn out to be useless they can learn to pass their input through unchanged, and
the model ends up no worse than a shallow one.

That is not what happens. Plain deep stacks get worse, and worse on the TRAINING
error, not just the test error. This rules out overfitting - an overfitting model
fits the training data better, not worse. The network is failing to optimize at
all.

1.1 Why The Signal Dies On The Way Back
---------------------------------------

Backpropagation carries the gradient from the loss at the top down to the weights
at the bottom by the chain rule, which multiplies one derivative per block:

    dLoss/dx_0 = dLoss/dx_N * dx_N/dx_(N-1) * ... * dx_1/dx_0

Each factor is typically a little below 1. Multiply 48 of them:

    0.9^48 = 0.006
    0.8^48 = 0.00002

The bottom layers receive almost nothing and barely move. Note 02 section 2.3
described the same arithmetic along the SEQUENCE: word 1 reaching word 50 through
49 updates. This is that failure along DEPTH: layer 1 reaching the loss through
48 blocks. Same product, different axis.

1.2 Why The Obvious Fixes Are Not Enough
----------------------------------------

Careful initialization sets those factors near 1 at step 0. It says nothing about
step 10,000, once the weights have moved.

Training longer does not rescue it either. A gradient arriving as 0.00002 does
not slowly accumulate into progress; it is swamped by the noise in the updates.

There is also a second problem underneath the gradient. For a plain deep stack to
be "no worse than shallow", the extra blocks have to learn the IDENTITY function.
A stack of matrix multiplications and non-linearities finds exact identity
surprisingly hard to represent. The architecture makes doing nothing expensive.


2. Residual Connections
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Add the block's input back to its output:

    output = x + Sublayer(x)

Sublayer is whatever the block does - multi-head attention (note 04) or the
feed-forward network (note 06). The addition is elementwise, and it creates a
second route through the block: one through the sublayer, one straight past it.

2.1 Why The Gradient Survives
-----------------------------

Differentiate the two routes together:

    d/dx [ x + F(x) ]  =  1 + F'(x)

The 1 is the entire point. Whatever F does - however small its derivative,
however badly it is initialized - the gradient still has a path of weight 1 back
to x. The chain over N blocks becomes a product of (1 + F') terms rather than a
product of small numbers, so it no longer collapses toward zero.

Every block now has a direct line to the loss, however deep it sits.

2.2 Why Doing Nothing Becomes Cheap
-----------------------------------

Section 1.2 said a plain block finds identity hard to learn. With a residual,
identity is the DEFAULT: driving F toward zero, which just means shrinking some
weights, leaves

    output = x + 0 = x

So a block starts near identity and learns a small correction on top of it. The
question it has to answer changes from "what should this layer compute?" to "what
should this layer CHANGE?", which is a far easier question. That is why adding
depth stops hurting.

2.3 The Shape Constraint It Imposes
-----------------------------------

x + Sublayer(x) only makes sense if both have the same shape. Every sublayer in
the model is therefore forced to map

    (batch, seq_len, d_model)  ->  (batch, seq_len, d_model)

This is not a coincidence in the design, it is a requirement the residual imposes
on everything it wraps. It is why multi-head attention ends by projecting back to
d_model with W_O (note 04 section 5), and why the feed-forward network expands to
d_ff and comes back down (note 06). Anything that changed the width would break
the add.

2.4 The Residual Stream
-----------------------

A useful way to picture a stack of these: there is one running vector per token,
d_model wide, travelling straight up through the whole model. Each sublayer READS
it, computes something, and ADDS the result back. Nothing is ever overwritten;
contributions accumulate.

    x_0  --> + attention(x_0) -->  x_1
         --> + ffn(x_1)       -->  x_2
         --> + attention(x_2) -->  x_3
         ...

This settles something note 03 left open. Positional encoding is added exactly
once, at the bottom of the stack. Layer 6 still knows where the words are because
the residual stream carried that signal upward: it was never overwritten, only
added to.


3. The Second Problem: Scale Drift
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Residuals make depth trainable, and introduce a side effect. Every block ADDS to
the stream and nothing ever subtracts, so the magnitude of the vectors grows as
they climb.

If a sublayer contributes something roughly the size of what is already there,
the stream can double in scale per block. Over 6 blocks that is a factor of 64,
and deeper stacks are far worse.

What large, drifting activations cost:

- Attention scores are dot products of these vectors, so they inflate with them.
  Note 02 section 5 showed exactly what oversized scores do to softmax:
  saturation, near one-hot weights, and gradients that stop flowing.
- A learning rate that suits layer 1 is wrong for layer 6, because the same step
  size means something different at a different scale.
- The scale keeps moving during training, so no fixed setting stays right.

The fix is to renormalize the stream at every block.


4. Why Not BatchNorm
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

BatchNorm is the standard answer in vision, and the natural first thing to try.
It normalizes each FEATURE across the BATCH: for feature j, collect its value in
all the examples of the batch, subtract that group's mean, divide by its standard
deviation.

It fails here for three separate reasons.

4.1 Padding Poisons The Statistics
----------------------------------

Sentences in a batch have different lengths, so the short ones are padded (note
02 section 7). Averaging a feature "across the batch" at position 40 means
averaging over whichever sentences happen to reach position 40, plus <PAD> slots
that carry no meaning at all. The statistics of a position depend on the
accidental length mix of the batch it landed in.

4.2 Training And Inference Disagree
-----------------------------------

At inference there is often no batch - you translate one sentence. BatchNorm
handles this by keeping running averages from training and using those instead.
That is already an approximation, and for text a poor one: the running statistics
for position 40 were collected from whatever subset of training sentences
happened to be that long.

4.3 It Couples Examples That Have Nothing To Do With Each Other
---------------------------------------------------------------

Under BatchNorm the output for sentence A depends on sentences B and C that
happen to share its batch. Reorder the batch and the numbers change. That is
tolerable when it acts as mild regularization, but it becomes fragile at the
small batch sizes large models train with, where the estimates are noisy.

The common thread is that all three problems come from normalizing ACROSS
EXAMPLES. So do not normalize across examples.


5. Layer Normalization
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

LayerNorm normalizes across the FEATURE axis instead: take one token's vector,
all d_model of its numbers, and normalize using only those.

    BatchNorm:  one feature, statistics gathered over the batch
    LayerNorm:  one token,   statistics gathered over its own features

No other token is involved, and no other sentence is involved. Every problem in
section 4 disappears at once: padding cannot pollute statistics it is not part
of, there is nothing to estimate during training and carry over to inference, and
a batch of 1 behaves exactly like a batch of 64.

5.1 The Formula
---------------

For a single token vector x with d_model components:

    mean       mu  = (1/d_model) * sum over i of x_i
    variance   var = (1/d_model) * sum over i of (x_i - mu)^2

    x_hat_i    = (x_i - mu) / sqrt(var + eps)

    output_i   = gamma_i * x_hat_i + beta_i

After the third line every token's vector has mean 0 and variance 1, whatever
scale it arrived with. Section 3's drift is removed at every block.

5.2 Why Epsilon
---------------

var can be exactly zero - a token whose components are all equal, which happens
with padding rows and with dead units. Dividing by sqrt(0) produces infinity or
NaN, and a single NaN spreads through the whole model in one step. eps, typically
1e-5 or 1e-6, is a floor that keeps the division safe, and is small enough to
change nothing otherwise.

5.3 Why Gamma And Beta
----------------------

Forcing mean 0 and variance 1 throws information away. If the best thing a layer
could do is output values centered on 3 with a spread of 10, plain normalization
forbids it.

gamma (a scale) and beta (a shift) are learned vectors of length d_model, one
entry per feature, that let the network put back whatever scale and offset it
wants:

    initialized   gamma = 1, beta = 0    the identity, so training begins with
                                         plain normalization
    learned       whatever the loss prefers

Normalization provides a stable starting point; gamma and beta let the model move
away from it deliberately, rather than drifting there by accident.

5.4 Shapes And Parameters
-------------------------

    input      (batch, seq_len, d_model)
    mu, var    (batch, seq_len, 1)        one number per token, per sentence
    gamma      (d_model,)                 shared by every token and sentence
    beta       (d_model,)
    output     (batch, seq_len, d_model)

Parameter count is 2 * d_model. At d_model = 512 that is 1,024 numbers per
LayerNorm, negligible beside the roughly 1 million in one attention block
(4 * d_model^2).

Note what is not shared: mu and var are recomputed for every token. Note what is:
gamma and beta are the same for all tokens in all sentences.

5.5 What It Does Not Do
-----------------------

LayerNorm never mixes tokens. Token 3's output depends on token 3's input and
nothing else. It is a per-token operation, exactly like the feed-forward network
of note 06. Only attention moves information across positions.

5.6 A Worked Example
--------------------

One token, d_model = 4:

    x = [ 2, 4, 4, 10 ]

    mu  = (2 + 4 + 4 + 10) / 4 = 5
    var = ( (-3)^2 + (-1)^2 + (-1)^2 + 5^2 ) / 4
        = ( 9 + 1 + 1 + 25 ) / 4 = 9
    sd  = 3

    x_hat = [ (2-5)/3, (4-5)/3, (4-5)/3, (10-5)/3 ]
          = [ -1.00, -0.33, -0.33, 1.67 ]

Checking: those four numbers have mean 0 and variance 1. With gamma = 1 and
beta = 0 that is the output. The vector arrived ranging from 2 to 10; whatever
scale the block below produced, what leaves here is standardized.


6. Putting Them Together: Add & Norm
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

6.1 The Paper's Order: Post-Norm
--------------------------------

    output = LayerNorm( x + Sublayer(x) )

Add first, then normalize the sum. This is what "Add & Norm" means in the paper's
figure, and what every box with that label in notes 07 and 10 refers to.

6.2 Where Dropout Goes
----------------------

The full expression actually trained is:

    output = LayerNorm( x + Dropout(Sublayer(x)) )

Dropout applies to what the sublayer produced, BEFORE the addition. It never
touches x itself: dropping the residual path would damage the identity route of
section 2.1, which is the one thing that has to work unconditionally. Note 11
covers dropout across the whole model.

6.3 The Modern Order: Pre-Norm
------------------------------

    output = x + Sublayer( LayerNorm(x) )

Normalize the sublayer's input, and add the raw x.

6.4 Why Pre-Norm Trains More Easily
-----------------------------------

The difference is where the LayerNorm sits relative to the residual path.

    post-norm   x --> [ + Sublayer ] --> LayerNorm --> out
                      the identity path passes THROUGH the LayerNorm

    pre-norm    x --> [ + Sublayer(LayerNorm(x)) ] --> out
                      the identity path is untouched

Under post-norm the clean 1 of section 2.1 is no longer clean. Every step
backwards also passes through a normalization, and across many layers those extra
factors compound. The residual highway has a toll booth on every block.

Under pre-norm, x reaches the output unmodified, so the gradient path back down
is exactly the identity however deep the stack gets.

The practical consequence: post-norm Transformers are hard to train without a
learning-rate warmup, and get harder as they get deeper. Pre-norm trains stably
at large depth and is much less sensitive to warmup. That is why the paper uses
post-norm and most models since do not.

Post-norm is not worthless. When it does train it often reaches slightly better
quality, which is why it survived in the original Transformer and in BERT.

6.5 Pre-Norm Needs A Final LayerNorm
------------------------------------

Under post-norm the last thing that happens in the stack is a normalization, so
whatever leaves the stack is normalized.

Under pre-norm every LayerNorm is applied to a sublayer's INPUT, so the value
that finally exits has been added to N times and never normalized afterwards -
which is exactly the drift of section 3. Pre-norm models therefore add ONE extra
LayerNorm at the top of the stack, after the last block. Leave it out and the
model still runs; it just trains worse.

6.6 Which To Implement
----------------------

Choose deliberately and record the choice in a comment, because the two are easy
to confuse in code and the wrong one produces a model that trains, only badly.

    following the paper exactly    post-norm
    following modern practice      pre-norm, plus a final LayerNorm

Be aware that widely used from-scratch implementations often write pre-norm while
describing the paper's diagram, so the code and the surrounding explanation
disagree. If an implementation computes x + dropout(sublayer(norm(x))), that is
pre-norm, whatever the text beside it says.


7. After The Paper: RMSNorm
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

LayerNorm does two things: it recenters, by subtracting the mean, and it
rescales, by dividing by the standard deviation. RMSNorm keeps only the second:

    rms(x)   = sqrt( (1/d_model) * sum over i of x_i^2  +  eps )
    output_i = gamma_i * x_i / rms(x)

No mean subtraction, and no beta.

The finding behind it is that the rescaling is what stabilizes training while the
recentering contributes little. Dropping it removes one pass over the vector and
one set of parameters, which is a real saving at scale.

Most models after roughly 2019, LLaMA and its descendants among them, use RMSNorm
with pre-norm placement. If you read a modern implementation expecting the paper's
LayerNorm, this is what you will find instead.


8. Where This Sits
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Add & Norm wraps EVERY sublayer in the model, so it appears:

    twice per encoder block     around attention, around the FFN      note 07
    three times per decoder     around masked attention, around
    block                       cross-attention, around the FFN       note 10

The sublayers it wraps are notes 04 and 06. The dropout inside it is note 11.


9. Practice Questions
--------------------------------------------------------------------------------

Q1: Deep plain networks score worse on TRAINING error than shallow ones. Why does
    that rule out overfitting, and what does it point to instead?
A:  Overfitting means fitting the training data too well, so it would show as good
    training error with poor test error. Worse training error means the optimizer
    is failing to find good weights at all - the gradient reaching the lower
    layers has collapsed through the product of many per-layer derivatives, and
    the extra blocks cannot easily represent the identity function that would
    make them harmless.

Q2: How does x + F(x) help the gradient? Answer with the derivative.
A:  d/dx [ x + F(x) ] = 1 + F'(x). The constant 1 is an unconditional path of
    weight 1 back to the input, so the chain across N blocks multiplies (1 + F')
    terms instead of small numbers and does not collapse. Every block keeps a
    direct route to the loss no matter how deep it is.

Q3: Why must every sublayer produce the same shape it received?
A:  Because the residual computes x + Sublayer(x) elementwise, which requires
    matching shapes. That constraint is why attention projects back to d_model
    with W_O and why the FFN expands to d_ff and returns to d_model.

Q4: Give two reasons BatchNorm is a poor fit for batches of sentences.
A:  Any two of: padded positions and varying sentence lengths mean the statistics
    at a position depend on the accidental composition of the batch; there is no
    batch at inference, so it must fall back on running averages collected from a
    different distribution; and it makes each sentence's output depend on the
    other sentences sharing its batch, which is noisy at small batch sizes. All
    three come from normalizing across examples, which LayerNorm avoids by
    normalizing across features within one token.

Q5: If the aim is mean 0 and variance 1, why add learnable gamma and beta that
    let the model undo it?
A:  Normalizing discards scale and offset that may be useful. gamma and beta let
    the network choose its own scale and offset deliberately, starting from 1 and
    0 so training begins at plain normalization. Normalization sets a stable
    default rather than a fixed rule.

Q6: What is the difference between post-norm and pre-norm, and what extra piece
    does pre-norm require?
A:  Post-norm is LayerNorm(x + Sublayer(x)), so the residual path runs through a
    normalization. Pre-norm is x + Sublayer(LayerNorm(x)), leaving the residual
    path untouched, which is why it trains stably at depth with little warmup.
    Pre-norm needs one extra LayerNorm after the final block, since nothing else
    would ever normalize the stack's output.

Q7: Positional encoding is added once, at the bottom. How does layer 6 still know
    about position?
A:  The residual connections carry it. Each sublayer adds its result into the
    running vector instead of replacing it, so the position signal injected at
    the bottom is never overwritten and is still present higher up.
